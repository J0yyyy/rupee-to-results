Rupee to Results

Every rupee a state spends on a child should end as a healthier child. This page follows one rupee through the system, meets the people who touch it, and shows the two questions the Task Force asks at every step.

₹ Money→Inputs→Outputs→Outcomes

Departments and posts named here are real. District and scheme numbers are illustrative, built to teach the logic, and marked on each chart.

A Lesson 1 · The whole idea in one breath

Money is not the goal. A child growing well is.

Think of a school tiffin. Your mother gives you ₹50 (money). You buy rice, dal and an egg (inputs). You eat the full meal (output). Over months you grow taller and stronger (outcome). If the ₹50 gets lost on the way, or buys chips instead of dal, the outcome never comes, even though "₹50 was spent".

The Task Force in one line: India's states already know this chain matters. They have frameworks. What they lack is a habit of using it when they actually decide where money goes. That is an adoption problem, not a framework problem.

B Lesson 2 · Meet the people

Six people touch one nutrition rupee

We'll follow nutrition money under Saksham Anganwadi & Poshan 2.0, the Centre–State scheme that pays for Anganwadi meals, Take-Home Ration (THR) and growth monitoring. In Maharashtra it is run by the Women & Child Development (WCD) Department through ICDS.

C Lesson 3 · Money moves in a circle, not a line

The budget loop

A budget is not made once in February and forgotten. It turns like a wheel, every year. A tool that only helps at one spoke stays on the side. The Task Force wants tools that help at every spoke.

D Lesson 4 · Two big questions

Two tracks, one purpose

Quantity Track
"Is the right amount of money reaching the right place at the right time?"

It's about flow. Faster to answer. Uses budget and treasury data.

Quality Track
"Is what we buy actually making children healthier?"

It's about effectiveness. Slower to answer. Needs many years and many states of data.

Sunita's Anganwadi can fail in two ways. Either the THR never arrives (a quantity problem), or it arrives every month but children still don't grow because THR alone isn't the best buy (a quality problem). You must check the first before you can judge the second.

E Lesson 5 · Quantity Track, lens 1

Allocative efficiency: is the money going where the need is?

Imagine five children at a table. One is very hungry, one is full. If you give equal rotis, or more rotis to the full one, the table is badly fed even though rotis were "distributed". Districts are the same.

How to read it: Nandurbar, Gadchiroli and Palghar (tribal, high-burden) carry about 74% of the stunting burden in this example but get 41% of the money. Pune and Mumbai Suburban get more than their share of need. The output of this lens is a judgement: "the structure of spending is not aligned with need."

The same lens also checks across departments. Nutrition is not only WCD: water and sanitation (Water Supply & Sanitation Dept), anaemia care (Public Health Dept), and the school mid-day meal (School Education Dept) all spend on the same child. Are they balanced?

G Lesson 7 · Quality Track

Three ways to ask "is this the best buy?"

Back to the tiffin. Before shopping, you can read what nutrition experts say about egg vs biscuits (Cost-Benefit). You can look at classmates whose mothers packed different tiffins and see who grew more (Comparative Effectiveness). Or you can watch your own child for a year (Value for Money). The last one only works if your child actually ate the tiffin every day.

Can start now

1 · Cost-Benefit Analysis

Which design gives more outcome per rupee? Uses evidence from other states and global studies. Works even if your own delivery is weak. Best used before money is committed.

Needs others' delivery

2 · Comparative Effectiveness

Compares input mixes across states or districts. Reliable only if the comparison districts actually spent their money.

Needs your delivery

3 · Value for Money (3 Es)

Economy: did we buy THR cheaply? Efficiency: did inputs become services? Effectiveness: did services move stunting? Meaningless if your own spending is erratic, which is exactly what the Quantity Track checks.

H Lesson 8 · From idea to tool

Frameworks don't run themselves

A recipe book doesn't cook. The Task Force's answer is codification: putting the thinking into two tools that officers can open during the meetings they already have.

Quantity Tool · like a thermometer

Tells you quickly if something is wrong and where: which districts are under-funded vs need, where funds stall, which months bunch.

Feeds: diagnostic reports, reallocation menus.

Quality Tool · like a report card

Tells you which interventions are earning their money and which are not, ranked by outcome per rupee.

Feeds: scheme redesign, next year's priorities.

Data plan: start with public data (budget documents, NFHS, Poshan Tracker dashboards), add government admin data as states share it, and fill gaps with clearly marked dummy data so officers see the full output and know what their own data would unlock.
